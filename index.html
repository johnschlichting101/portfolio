Key takeaways

  • Designed for a safe first flight. Sized the vertical tail to favor a correctable spiral over Dutch roll, and accepted ~18% static margin over the 15% target.
  • Built every part of the tail myself, from CNC cut NACA 0012 foam surfaces to a basswood and carbon fiber empennage box.

What I did

  • Sized both stabilizers with volume coefficients: VH = 0.685, VV = 0.0403
  • Set CG at 0.43 m against a neutral point at 0.487 m over a 0.315 m MAC
  • Fabricated NACA 0012 surfaces surfaces on a 4-axis CNC foam cutter
  • Built the basswood and carbon fiber empennage box by hand

The call I made

I designed the tail to favor spiral instability over Dutch roll. A spiral is a slow, tightening descending turn, and a pilot can fix it with basic stick input. Dutch roll is an out of phase yaw and roll oscillation, and it's much harder to recover from. To get there, I sized the vertical stabilizer toward the upper end of the recommended range, since more vertical tail area strengthens directional stability and pushes the aircraft toward spiral.

Result

  • Three successful flights, ending with the first in-flight MTE deflection
  • Wind tunnel: over 100% CL increase at 0° AoA, neutral to 30° MTE
  • Over 30% CL increase at 5° AoA, significant across all deflections

Both tail volumes sit high in their ranges on purpose. VH and VV give strong pitch and yaw authority and extra stability, and high VV pushes the aircraft toward a correctable spiral over Dutch roll. A tip mounted camera requirement, pushed VV higher and put more weight aft, so I used a 0.6 taper ratio on the vertical stabilizer to help reduce weight and induced drag.

Design notes

Every tail decision moves the CG

A bigger horizontal stabilizer buys pitch authority but adds weight far aft, eroding static margin. A bigger vertical guards against Dutch roll at the same penalty. I sized both against light aircraft ranges: VH 0.685 in 0.4–0.8 and VV 0.0403 in 0.02–0.05.

Why 18% and not 15%

Too stable and the aircraft goes sluggish, damping the pitch response we needed to measure MTE effects. Too little and gusts cause outsized pitch reactions on an untested airframe. The extra margin was a deliberate trade.

Airfoil

NACA 0012 on both surfaces, symmetric, so zero pitching moment at zero AoA, and well documented at our low Reynolds numbers.

Structure

Lifting surface spars pass through and intersect the main carbon spar rather than stopping at it, improving load transfer in bending and torsion. The CNC cut spar channels in the same pass as the airfoil, so their locations were exact. Control surfaces hang on continuous living hinges.

A gap I caught in assembly

I hadn’t allocated a servo mount. I added a basswood block forward of the empennage box for a rigid mount with short, direct linkages.

Wind wall validation

Custom load cell stand at Re ≈ 155,000. Over 100% CL increase at 0° AoA from neutral to 30° MTE, and over 30% at 5° AoA, statistically significant across all deflection angles.

Fixed wing glider

Aerospace Vehicle Design Lab, Summer 2024, team project

We designed, built, and hand-launched a 1 m fixedwing glider with a goal of gliding 100 m from a 14.5 m height. Our glider fell well short of our model's prediction, and this is where I learned the difference between prediction models and reality

Why it fell short

  • Our glider stalled on nearly every flight due to the trim AoA being too high, airspeed too low
  • Launch angle and velocity weren’t in the model, and they decided whether it entered a stable glide
  • The analysis wasn’t wrong. It was incomplete and I carried this mindset into future projects.

What we did

  • MATLAB sensitivity sweep on every major sizing parameter, doubling or halving each until range peaked, then bisecting to the optimum. Wing area dominated range, so we maximized it up to the 1 m span limit, and we kept the fuselage small and light
  • Drag polar by component buildup (CD0 = 0.0396) with the Obert model for span efficiency, used to find max L/D for range and the different speed for max endurance
  • 3D lift curve corrected from 2D Eppler 393 data, with airfoil data matched to our expected Reynolds number

The call we made

We researched several configurations, including a twin fuselage modeled on the Virgin GlobalFlyer and a high aspect ratio sailplane modeled on the AS 33. Both were too complex to fabricate and model within our 1 m span limit and 10 week schedule. We chose a conventional high wing and T-tail, a layout based on well performing gliders that we could build, modify, and model with confidence.

Iteration

Initial configuration testing showed a nose dive with a fixed tail. We added adjustable tail incidence and found ~5° gave trimmed flight through test flights.

Design notes

Configuration

The high wing puts the aerodynamic center above the CG for a passive roll restoring effect. The T-tail keeps the stabilizer out of the wing and fuselage wake, so a smaller surface gives stable pitch authority. Natural stability, no active control.

Sensitivity method

For each parameter we doubled or halved an estimate until range overshot its peak, then bisected toward the optimum. Wing area drove range most; fuselage weight and frontal area hurt it; taper, sweep, and stabilizer planform barely moved it, so we set those for easy fabrication.

Stability

Neutral point at 0.12 m from the leading edge, with CG placed forward for a static margin inside the required 10–30%.

Flight test method

Flights at several tail incidence angles let us map the drag polar experimentally, computing CL and CD from measured range and comparing against the model. Average flight time was about 8 seconds.

Key takeaways

  • Started from user research. 68% of cane users we surveyed are active fewer than 2 days a week.
  • One hard requirement, no behavior change. All electronics hide inside the handle without changing its profile.
  • Built to manufacture, with an aluminum shaft, TPU foot, ASA printed housing, and Arduino step counter.

Design and business notes

Research

Interviews with older adults, caregivers, and physical therapists. 75% of caregivers believed their loved ones would benefit from more activity, and 55% of that activity already came from walking. Patent and competitor review covered 10+ products.

Requirements

Accurate step count, lightweight, easy to use, durable, good looking, and easy battery replacement. Interviews made the walnut handle a must as our users wanted something that looked premium, not medical.

Market

$28B TAM in global elderly and assistive devices, $532M SAM in U.S. canes, and ~$106M SOM from the 20% of cane users willing to adopt a wearable health device.

Manufacturing

Machine the shaft, shape the handle, print the housing, solder, assemble. About 1.5 hours of hands on labor and 5 hours total per unit, with every material chosen for repeatability at scale.

Cord Keeper Pro

Product Design, Fall 2024, team of five

Labor and delivery nurses stored four $5,000 NICU sensors in a plastic bag, and the cords tangled at the moment of highest clinical demand. We built a wall mounted unit that retracts each cord automatically for easier organization.

Team result

Delivered a working prototype on schedule despite supply chain delays.

Key takeaways

  • Requirements came from a nurse interview: portable, organized, no learning curve, cheap enough to replace a familiar system.
  • Pull to retrieve, release to retract. Four independent cylinders, one per sensor.
  • Our clock springs never arrived before the expo. We quickly switched to elastic cord on bearings and still delivered a working prototype.
  • Every first time tester used it correctly with no instructions.

Engineering notes

Original mechanism

A custom clock spring stores energy as the cord is pulled and drives the cylinder back on release. We designed the whole unit around it.

The pivot

With the springs missing, a teammate proposed elastic cord wrapped around bearings inside each cylinder. Pulling the sensor stretches the elastic; releasing it rotates the cylinder and rewinds the cord. Elastic degrades over time, so production would need a different part, but it proved the concept completely.

Validation testing

Support reactions and ideal pull angles for the wall mount; magnet pull and adhesion; velcro hold; elastic elongation, durability, and cycling strength; rubber flexibility and friction for consistent cord grip.

Usability

We handed it to people who had never seen it and gave no instructions. Every participant operated it successfully. Succesfully acheiving our most important requirement.

About me

I'm a CU Boulder graduate in Integrated Design Engineering (IDE), with an Aerospace concentration and an Engineering Management emphasis. IDE is a design centered engineering degree built around team projects: you start from a user need, work through analysis and hardware, and finish with a business case. That meant owning five projects end to end, and it's why I think about a part's manufacturability and cost as early as its performance.

Aviation runs in my family. My great grandfather was a lead machinist in Pratt and Whitney's experimental shop in the 1940s, and my grandfather, a retired Air Force lieutenant general, was the program director for the B-2 Spirit and F-117 Nighthawk programs. They have set the bar high and I'm here to meet it.
